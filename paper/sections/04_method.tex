\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We extract the ESG passages of each report (\S\ref{sec:method-extraction}),
count in them the mentions of a documented ESG vocabulary
(\S\ref{sec:method-vocab}) and combine tone, substance, quantification and
hedging into two scores (\S\ref{sec:method-index}); \S\ref{sec:method-choices}
explains the design and \S\ref{sec:method-inference} the panel inference.

\subsection{Extraction of ESG passages}
\label{sec:method-extraction}

The reports of \S\ref{sec:data} are annual reports and universal registration
documents, in which ESG disclosure is embedded in a much larger financial and
legal document. Scored whole, such a document would let financial-statement
bulk and document length dominate every component. We therefore first extract
its ESG passages; every later measure is computed on them alone.

Each PDF is read as text blocks in reading order, and filters keep only running
prose. Page furniture is dropped: blocks entirely within the top or bottom
8\,\% of the page, and short margin blocks that recur, digits aside, on more
than 30\,\% of a document's pages (running headers and footers). Tabular blocks
(a high share of digits in a few lines, or short lines of which the first lacks
terminal punctuation) are dropped unless they contain a vocabulary match. Short
all-capital blocks are taken as section headings, which label the paragraphs
that follow instead of entering the text. Navigation text (tables of contents,
GRI and ESRS correspondence tables, stray page references), recognised by
dotted leaders, trailing page numbers, multi-level section numbers and
disclosure codes, is dropped, because such lists of ESG section names would
otherwise pass the density rule below. Paragraphs are then stripped of web
addresses, rejoined across line-break hyphenation and collapsed to single
spaces.

Each paragraph is matched against the vocabulary of \S\ref{sec:method-vocab}
on its unlemmatised text, ignoring case, with spaces and hyphens
interchangeable between the words of a phrase and longer entries matched before
the shorter ones they contain, so that \emph{carbon footprint} counts once. A
paragraph with at least one match per 100 words is retained together with one
paragraph on each side; overlapping or adjacent windows are merged into a
single zone, and zones shorter than 150 characters are discarded. The zones of
a report, in document order, form its extracted text. Across the 343 reports,
extraction kept 66,591 zones holding 28.3 million of the 71.4 million words of
the PDF text layers, 39.7\,\% (per report, median 38.6\,\%, range
7.1--75.6\,\%). The share rises over the window, from a mean of 31.6\,\% of a
report in 2018 to 49.6\,\% in 2024: ESG disclosure takes a growing part of the
documents. The procedure depends only on the PDF and the vocabulary, and its
recall is bounded by the vocabulary: a passage that uses none of its entries
is never selected.

\subsection{ESG vocabulary}
\label{sec:method-vocab}

The vocabulary defines what counts as an ESG mention, both for selecting
passages and for measuring substance, so it is documented in full. Candidate
entries came from three sources: the disclosure topics and terminology of
reporting frameworks and standards (GRI, SASB, TCFD and the ESRS), including
the names of the frameworks and regulatory regimes under which firms label
their disclosure; the ESG vocabulary of dictionary-based studies of corporate
disclosure; and a structured review of sample reports from the corpus, in which
independent readers listed the ESG terms they found and independent evaluators
judged the pooled list.
\textbf{[Author: identify the reviewers of the candidate lists.]}
A candidate was admitted if its occurrences in the extracted text, read in
context, predominantly carry an ESG sense (a disclosure, target, data point or
policy on an environmental, social or governance matter). Candidates from the
review had in addition to be dispersed across firms, with at least 50
occurrences in the reports of at least 5 of the 49 firms. A polysemous head was
admitted only inside enumerated collocations (\emph{carbon footprint} rather
than \emph{footprint}; the ESG collocations of \emph{social} and \emph{water}),
and a morphological family or a term carrying digits was written as a pattern
(every word beginning \emph{sustainab-} or \emph{recycl-}; CO$_2$e; Scope 1--3;
ISO standard numbers). Each entry belongs to one pillar: environmental (E),
social (S), governance (G), or transversal (TRANS) for umbrella terms and
reporting regimes (\emph{sustainability}, \emph{ESG}, \emph{CSRD}). Entries
whose occurrences concentrate in one industry are designated sectoral; they are
part of the vocabulary, and excluding them is a sensitivity check
(\S\ref{sec:robustness}). Table~\ref{tab:method-vocab} gives the composition.

Terms were admitted after reviewing a sample of their occurrences in context,
and the frequency-weighted precision of the resulting vocabulary is estimated
from those samples (3,401 occurrences of 430 entries), not measured directly
on the final extraction, at about 82\,\% (95\,\% interval 80--84\,\%). The stability of the scores under perturbations of the vocabulary is
reported in \S\ref{sec:robustness}.

One vocabulary drives both extraction and scoring: all 434 entries select the
passages, and the 402 terms, lemmatised, are what the substance component
counts. Changing the vocabulary therefore changes the text on which every
component is computed, not only the substance count. For scoring, the extracted
text is lowercased, tokenised and lemmatised with a standard English language
model; stopwords (the standard list and 189 corpus-specific words),
punctuation, numerals and tokens shorter than three characters are removed, and
the vocabulary passes through the same processing, so that each entry is
matched in the representation of the text it is counted on. Eleven phrases that
this processing would empty or reduce to a generic word (\emph{well-being},
both of whose words are stopwords; \emph{say on pay} to \emph{pay};
\emph{ISO 14001} to \emph{iso}) are first replaced by single protected tokens,
in text and vocabulary alike. The 402 terms yield $V = 399$ distinct
lemmatised scoring entries.

\subsection{Components and scores}
\label{sec:method-index}

Each score combines a measure of how positively a report speaks about ESG
matters with measures of how much ESG content it provides. Let $d = 1, \dots,
n$ index the $n = 343$ reports and $j = 1, \dots, V$ the scoring entries. Each
report has two versions of its extracted text: the \emph{lemmatised text} of
\S\ref{sec:method-vocab}, with $L_d$ tokens, and the \emph{raw text}, with
case, digits and stopwords intact, with $R_d$ tokens. Let $c_{dj}$ be the
number of occurrences of entry $j$ in the lemmatised text of $d$, counted over
word sequences of one to four tokens (the length of the longest entry). Entries
are counted independently, so an occurrence of a multi-word entry also counts
for any shorter entry it contains.

\paragraph{Substance.} $\SUS$ is the density of ESG mentions, the number of
vocabulary occurrences per 100 lemmatised tokens, with every entry weighted
equally:
\[
\SUS_d \;=\; \susdens_d \;=\; 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{j} c_{dj}}{L_d}.
\]
The superscript, used in tables and figures, marks the density specification
as distinct from the alternatives examined in \S\ref{sec:robustness}.

\paragraph{Tone.} $\SEN$ is the Loughran--McDonald polarity
\citep{loughran2011} of the raw text,
\[
\SEN_d \;=\; \frac{s^{+}_d - s^{-}_d}{s^{+}_d + s^{-}_d},
\]
where $s^{+}_d$ and $s^{-}_d$ count the words of the text, lowercased, that
appear as written in the dictionary's positive (354 forms) and negative (2,355
forms) lists. The lists are lists of inflected forms and are matched as such,
without stemming either side, which would admit words the dictionary does not
contain (\emph{objectives} or \emph{diversity} as negative, \emph{information}
as positive). Following \citet{loughran2011}, a positive word preceded within
three words by a negation (\emph{no}, \emph{not}, \emph{none},
\emph{neither}, \emph{never}, \emph{nobody}) is not counted. $\SEN$ ranges from
$-1$ (only negative words) to $1$ (only positive words) and depends on the
balance of positive and negative words, not on how many there are.

\paragraph{Quantification.} A report that discloses substance can also put
numbers on it. $\QUANT$ counts such evidence on the raw text, because
preprocessing removes numerals:
\[
\QUANT_d \;=\; m_d / R_d,
\]
where $m_d$ is the number of matches of three pattern families: percentages;
numbers of four or more digits that are magnitudes, excluding years, year
ranges, standard codes (ISO 14001) and the numbers of legal acts; and physical
units of mass, energy, power and volume. Only figures count: the names of
reporting frameworks are not quantification and grow with reporting mandates,
and \emph{CO$_2$} or \emph{GHG} without a figure are topic words that the
substance component already counts.

\paragraph{Hedging.} Qualified language (\emph{may}, \emph{could},
\emph{uncertain}) weakens whatever a report asserts. The hedging component is
the share of hedging words in the raw text,
\[
\HEDGE_d \;=\; h_d / W_d,
\]
where $W_d$ is the number of words (letter runs, hyphenated compounds kept
whole) and $h_d$ the number of them in the Uncertainty and Weak Modal lists of
the Loughran--McDonald master dictionary, 295 word forms taken as written. The
Strong Modal and Constraining lists (\emph{must}, \emph{will},
\emph{required}) express commitment or obligation rather than qualification,
and \emph{risk} and \emph{risks}, although in the Uncertainty list, are left
out: naming a risk is disclosure that frameworks require, not a qualification
of a claim. \emph{May} is not counted where it names the month. $\HEDGE$ is
computed on the raw text because the modal verbs (\emph{may}, \emph{might},
\emph{could}) are stopwords. It measures reserved language in general, not
the vagueness of commitments: it does not track the share of non-specific
climate commitments found by an independent classifier
(\S\ref{sec:robustness}).

\paragraph{Pillars.} For description only, the pillar density counts the
mentions of a pillar's entries per 100 lemmatised tokens, so that the four
pillar densities sum to $\SUS_d$; for a set of reports, the pillar share pools
the mentions of the set. Neither enters a score.

\paragraph{Scores.} Each component $x$ is rescaled across the corpus by a
$z$-score, $\Z(x)_d = (x_d - \bar{x}) / \sigma_x$, with $\bar x$ the mean and
$\sigma_x$ the population standard deviation over all 343 reports pooled across
the seven years, so that yearly means of a score are comparable. The two
scores are
\begin{align}
\label{eq:esgsi}
\ESGSI_d    &= \Z(\SEN)_d - \Z(\SUS)_d, \\[2pt]
\label{eq:esgsiext}
\ESGSIext_d &= \Z(\SEN)_d - \Z(\SUS)_d - w_q\,\Z(\QUANT)_d + w_h\,\Z(\HEDGE)_d,
\qquad w_q = w_h = 0.5 .
\end{align}
$\ESGSI$~\eqref{eq:esgsi} is the main score of the paper: it follows the
architecture of \citet{lagasio2024}, tone minus substance with each rescaled
across the sample, with the components defined above. A higher value means
more positive tone relative to the ESG content of the text. The Extended
ESGSI~\eqref{eq:esgsiext} is the new metric this paper proposes. Quantified
disclosure is further evidence of substance, so $\Z(\QUANT)$ is subtracted, on
the same side as $\Z(\SUS)$; hedging qualifies whatever a report asserts, so
$\Z(\HEDGE)$ is added, on the side of tone. The weights give each added
component half the weight of an original one, so that together they weigh as
much as one of them and the tone--substance contrast remains the core of the
score. They are a stated choice, not an estimate, since no external criterion
is available to calibrate them; \S\ref{sec:robustness} reports the scores over
a grid of weights.

\subsection{Design choices}
\label{sec:method-choices}

The score contrasts two positions in the corpus: where a report stands on
tone and where it stands on the density of its ESG content. It is a contrast of
relative positions, not a residual. It does not model the tone that a report's
substance would predict, and could not usefully do so here: the two components
are only weakly correlated (0.20 pooled, 95\,\% interval $-0.03$ to $0.41$
from resampling firms; 0.11 within firms and 0.25 between firm means), so tone
net of substance is almost tone itself. Each component is chosen for that
contrast.

Substance is an amount per unit of text. Counting ESG mentions per 100 words
means that a long report does not score more substance merely for being long,
so reports of very different length are comparable; and since the count uses
no statistics of the other reports, a report's substance does not change when
the sample does. As a check of convergent validity in the sense of
\citet{campbell1959}, substance should move with quantification, which is
counted with patterns on the raw text rather than with the vocabulary. Measures
that keep the amount of disclosure agree with it to a similar degree (0.45 for
density, 95\,\% interval 0.27--0.60 from resampling firms; 0.46 for a TF-IDF
count divided by length), while a TF-IDF specification that scales each
report's term vector to unit length, and so discards the amount, agrees much
less (0.22). The agreement is mainly between firms (0.60 across firm means,
0.23 within firms), and it holds when the entries that both measures count are
removed from the vocabulary (0.38). Since $\QUANT$ is also a component of
$\ESGSIext$, this is a check on substance, not on the score.
\S\ref{sec:robustness} reports the scores under these alternative
specifications. Tone is read with the
Loughran--McDonald dictionary because it was built from corporate filings and
classifies words by the sense they carry in that register \citep{loughran2011}.

Tone and substance come in different units, a bounded ratio and mentions per
100 words, so each is standardised before they are combined: a $z$-score
expresses a report's value in standard deviations from the corpus mean, and
the difference in \eqref{eq:esgsi} is then a difference of relative positions
on a common scale. A score is measured in standard deviations of its
components, not of itself: its own standard deviation is 1.27 for $\ESGSI$
and 1.53 for $\ESGSIext$, and slopes are reported on the component scale. The
scale has no natural zero. Zero is the corpus mean of
the combination, and no external benchmark exists against which a boundary
could be calibrated for these reports (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). We therefore
read each score as a relative position and apply no threshold: we compare
positions within the corpus through differences of means, trends, rank
correlations and the overlap of the top and bottom deciles. The Extended ESGSI
adds the two features of disclosure that the tone--substance contrast leaves
out, whether substance is quantified and whether claims are qualified.

\subsection{Inference on the panel}
\label{sec:method-inference}

A firm's seven reports resemble each other from year to year, so treating them
as 343 independent observations would overstate precision. We therefore
estimate trends within firms and measure their uncertainty at the level of the
firm. Write $d = (i, t)$ for the report of firm $i = 1, \dots, M$ in year $t$,
with $M = 49$. The trend is the slope of a score on calendar year with firm
fixed effects, which compares each firm only with itself; in the balanced
panel it equals the average of the firms' own slopes. Standard errors are
clustered by firm, with the usual small-sample correction and a $t$
distribution with $M - 1$ degrees of freedom: they allow any correlation among
a firm's reports, but not a shock common to all firms in one year, which seven
years are too few to account for, so the slope summarises the yearly means.
Because 49 firms are a modest number of clusters, we also compute $p$-values
by a wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008}, which rebuilds the data under
a zero trend by flipping the sign of all of a firm's residuals together ($+1$
or $-1$ with equal probability, $B = 9{,}999$ replications); the $p$-value is
the share of replications whose clustered $t$ statistic is at least as large
in absolute value as the observed one.

For an industry or country, the slope is the average of its firms' slopes.
With few firms, the clustered standard error of a group slope is too narrow,
so we read it through the $t$ interval on the group's $M_k$ firm-level slopes
($M_k - 1$ degrees of freedom). Equality of slopes across groups is tested
with a Wald test on the clustered variance, which overstates significance when
a group has a single firm. Yearly means carry 95\,\% intervals from a
regression on year indicators, clustered by firm.
