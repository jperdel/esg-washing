\section{The ESG vocabulary}
\label{sec:lexicon}

This section presents the ESG vocabulary, how it was built and how it is
brought into the same representation as the corpus, together with the hedge
lexicon used by \HEDGE. The vocabulary has 434 entries: 402 natural-form terms
and 32 regular-expression patterns. Of these, 36 (35 terms and one pattern)
are designated sectoral; they are part of the vocabulary used throughout the
paper, and excluding them is a sensitivity check
(\S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon}). The source of truth is
\nolinkurl{metadata/ESG_terms_v3.csv}, one row per entry with its type,
pillar and sectoral flag; every count below is recomputed from committed files
by \nolinkurl{scripts/vocab_v3_stats.py}. \citet{lagasio2024} does not publish
its keyword list or its size; ours is published in full so that every
inclusion can be inspected.

\subsection{Sources of candidate terms}
\label{sec:lexicon-sources}

Candidates came from three sources.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Reporting frameworks and standards.} The disclosure topics and
  terminology of GRI, SASB, TCFD and the ESRS, including the names of the
  frameworks and regulatory regimes themselves (\emph{csrd}, \emph{tcfd},
  \emph{eu taxonomy}), which firms use to organise and label their ESG
  disclosure.
\item \emph{Prior literature.} ESG terminology from dictionary-based studies of
  corporate disclosure, which supplied the general environmental, social and
  governance vocabulary (\emph{emission}, \emph{human rights},
  \emph{remuneration}).
\item \emph{A structured review of sample reports.} The extracted zones of 17
  reports from the corpus (13 firms, 7 countries) were read by three
  reviewers, working independently and without sight of the vocabulary, each
  of whom listed every ESG term found. After normalising case, plurals,
  hyphenation and spelling (\nolinkurl{scripts/build_consensus.py}), the pooled
  list was given in random order to three further evaluators, also working
  independently and without knowing how many reviewers had listed each term
  (\nolinkurl{scripts/build_consensus_v2.py}). Each evaluator judged, for every
  term, whether it is an ESG term, its pillar, whether it is sector-specific,
  and whether it needs a collocation to fix its sense. A term entered the
  candidate pool from this source only if all three evaluators approved it.
  Germany, 98 of the 343 reports, is represented in the sample by a single
  firm. \textbf{[Author: identify the reviewers of the candidate lists.]}
\end{enumerate}
The review supplements the first two sources with the vocabulary firms
actually use; its reading sample is small, and absence from it says nothing
against a term.

\subsection{Admission criteria}
\label{sec:lexicon-criteria}

A candidate, from any source, was admitted only if it met the following
criteria.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{ESG sense in context.} Its occurrences in the extracted text of
  this corpus, read in context, predominantly carry the ESG sense: a
  disclosure, target, data point or policy on an environmental, social or
  governance matter. A word whose occurrences are dominated by a non-ESG use
  in these reports is not admitted as a bare term: \emph{materiality}
  (accounting and audit materiality), \emph{fuel} (the verb), \emph{ecosystem}
  (business or digital ecosystems), \emph{footprint} (industrial or
  geographic footprint), \emph{offset} (netting, \emph{partially offset by}).
\item \emph{Dispersion across firms.} A candidate from the review of sample
  reports had to occur at least 50 times in the corpus and in the reports of
  at least 5 of the 49 firms (constants \texttt{MIN\_OCC\_CANDIDATE} and
  \texttt{MIN\_COMPANIES\_CANDIDATE} in
  \nolinkurl{scripts/build_kwic_sample.py}). The cut keeps out terms that are
  idiosyncratic to one or two firms; it is a practical choice, not a
  statistical test.
\item \emph{Collocations for polysemous heads.} A head that carries the ESG
  sense only in some combinations is admitted only inside enumerated
  collocations: \emph{carbon footprint} rather than \emph{footprint}, the
  collocations of \emph{social} and \emph{water}, and
  \texttt{environmental\textbackslash w*} and \emph{environmentally} rather
  than every word beginning \emph{environ-}. A count vocabulary cannot carry
  a negative lookahead (``\emph{social}, except \emph{social security}''), so
  a polysemous head is either admitted bare or admitted only in the
  collocations listed; the second option gives up the head's other valid uses
  in exchange for excluding its invalid ones. A collocation inherits the
  pillar and sectoral designation of its head. The vocabulary holds 82
  collocations.
\item \emph{Patterns for morphological families and digit-bearing forms.}
  Where a family of forms shares a stem (\texttt{sustainab\textbackslash w+},
  \texttt{recycl\textbackslash w+}), a term carries digits that preprocessing
  removes (\texttt{co2e?}, \texttt{scope\textbackslash s*[123]}, ISO standard
  numbers), or a phrase does not survive lemmatisation
  (\texttt{paris\textbackslash s+agreement}, \emph{just transition}), the entry
  is written as a regular expression. Patterns act at extraction only
  (\S\ref{sec:lexicon-one}). There are 32.
\item \emph{Sectoral designation.} An entry whose occurrences are concentrated
  in one industry is admitted but designated sectoral, because counting it
  would reward a firm for belonging to that industry rather than for what it
  discloses. The designation rests on two criteria: concentration of the
  entry's occurrences across all 343 reports, including the share held by the
  single largest firm, and, for candidates from the review of sample reports,
  a majority of the evaluators who approved the term judging it
  sector-specific. The exact concentration thresholds of the first criterion
  are not in a committed script. Collocations inherit the designation of their
  head.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pillar assignment}
\label{sec:lexicon-pillars}

Every entry has exactly one pillar: E (environmental), S (social), G
(governance), or TRANS (transversal) for umbrella terms that name ESG as a
whole or its reporting regimes (\emph{sustainability}, \emph{esg},
\emph{csrd}). Candidates from the review of sample reports take the pillar most
often given by the evaluators who approved them; collocations take their
head's. The index pools the pillars (\S\ref{sec:index}); the assignment matters
only for the leave-one-pillar-out stability tests
(\S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon}) and the pillar breakdowns of
\S\ref{sec:temporal}. The EU taxonomy family (\emph{eu taxonomy},
\emph{taxonomy eligible}, \emph{taxonomy alignment}) is assigned to TRANS, as a
reporting regime, although the regulation's six objectives are all
environmental; that assignment is a choice and could reasonably be E.

\subsection{One vocabulary for extraction and scoring}
\label{sec:lexicon-one}

The vocabulary does two jobs. Matched as regular expressions on raw text, its
entries decide which paragraphs are extracted (\S\ref{sec:extraction});
lemmatised, its terms are what \SUS\ counts (\S\ref{sec:index}). The 32
patterns act only at the first stage. Changing the vocabulary therefore
changes the corpus as well as the score: because the same entries select the
zones, even \SEN, which never consults the ESG vocabulary, is computed on
different text when the vocabulary changes.

We use one vocabulary for both jobs rather than a broad extraction list paired
with a narrower scoring list, because every component is computed on the
extracted text. A zone selected by a term that fails the admission criteria
adds non-disclosure prose to \SEN, inflates the denominator of \susdens, and
adds accounting figures to \QUANT\ and modal verbs to \HEDGE. The criteria of
\S\ref{sec:lexicon-criteria} therefore apply to extraction and scoring alike.
The consequence for evaluation is that every stability test either freezes the
extraction or simulates re-extraction (\S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon}).

\subsection{Section-naming terms and navigation text}
\label{sec:lexicon-nav}

Tables of contents and GRI/ESRS correspondence tables are lists of section
names, and many section names are vocabulary entries (\emph{governance},
\emph{esrs}, \emph{tcfd}); they are dense in ESG keywords without being
disclosure. Removing the section-naming terms would lose their valid uses in
prose, so the vocabulary keeps them and the navigation text is removed instead
by a filter in the extractor (\S\ref{sec:extraction-nav}). The noise that
comes from the kind of text selected is handled at extraction; the vocabulary
is judged on the sense of its terms in running text.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Every zone identified in \S\ref{sec:extraction} passes through one
\texttt{TextProcessor}: spaCy's \texttt{en\_core\_web\_md}, with named-entity
recognition and parsing disabled. URLs are stripped; text is lowercased;
eleven protected vocabulary phrases are replaced by single tokens
(\S\ref{sec:lexicon-represent}); the text is then tokenised and lemmatised. A
token survives if it is not a stopword, punctuation or whitespace, is longer
than two characters, its lemma is not one of 189 corpus-specific stopwords,
and it is alphanumeric with at least two letters (keeping \texttt{co2} and
\texttt{sf6}, dropping \texttt{2023} and \texttt{p12}). The lemmatised string
(\texttt{clean\_text}) feeds \SUS, \SEN\ and \BREADTH; a whitespace-collapsed
copy of the extracted text with digits and case intact (\texttt{raw\_text}) is
the input to \QUANT\ and \HEDGE, which need the numerals and the stopwords
respectively.

\subsection{Vocabulary and corpus in one representation}
\label{sec:lexicon-represent}

The scoring vocabulary is generated, not written by hand.
\nolinkurl{scripts/export_vocab_v3.py} writes the 434 entries of
\nolinkurl{metadata/ESG_terms_v3.csv} into four source files (terms or
patterns, sectoral or not: 367, 35, 31 and 1 entries), and
\nolinkurl{scripts/build_lexicons.py} passes every term through the same
\texttt{TextProcessor} as the corpus. A vocabulary entry and its occurrences in
the corpus are therefore in one representation by construction: no entry can
fail to match for want of a shared tokenisation or lemmatisation. The loader
warns when a source file is newer than its generated file and stops when the
generated file is missing.

\paragraph{Protected phrases.} Preprocessing would destroy some phrases.
\emph{Well-being} would be emptied, as both of its words are spaCy stopwords;
\emph{iso 14001} would become \emph{iso} and \emph{say on pay} would become
\emph{pay}, a generic word that would count every unrelated use. The build
therefore first lemmatises every term with a processor that has no protection;
each term that comes out empty, and each multi-word term that comes out as a
single word and is not on a reviewed allow-list, is written to
\nolinkurl{metadata/esg_protected_phrases.txt} as a pair: a regular expression
and a single token. The regular expression accepts the words separated by
spaces, hyphens or nothing, plus an optional final \emph{s}. The token is the
term with its non-alphanumeric characters removed and any leading digits moved
to the end (\emph{well-being} $\to$ \emph{wellbeing}, \emph{2030 agenda}
$\to$ \emph{agenda2030}). \texttt{TextProcessor} applies the substitutions to
the lowercased text before tokenising, for the corpus and for the vocabulary
alike, so a protected term and its occurrences meet in the same token. Eleven
phrases qualify (Table~\ref{tab:lexicon-lemma}), and thirteen vocabulary terms
are represented by the eleven protected tokens: the unhyphenated
\emph{wellbeing} and the fused \emph{cop28} share the token of their spaced
variants. spaCy may lemmatise the token itself (\emph{wellbeing} $\to$
\emph{wellbee}), which is harmless because both sides are lemmatised alike;
the build stops if a token does not survive as one word. Two collapses are on
the allow-list and stand without protection, \emph{due diligence} $\to$
\emph{diligence} and \emph{eu taxonomy} $\to$ \emph{taxonomy}, on the judgement
that the surviving word occurs almost only inside the phrase in these reports.

\paragraph{N-gram structure.} Every one of the 402 terms reaches \SUS, as one
of $V = 399$ lemmatised entries. Multi-word entries are 259 of the 399
(64.9\%); the longest has four tokens, so the vectoriser's
\texttt{ngram\_range} is (1,4), derived at run time from the vocabulary.
Under scikit-learn's unigram default every multi-word entry would score zero,
without an error.

\paragraph{Extraction and scoring, one entry.} Stopword and punctuation
removal makes tokens adjacent that were not, so a lemmatised multi-word entry
can match more than its phrase: \emph{board director}, the lemma of
\emph{board of directors}, also matches \emph{board and directors}. The excess
is small. On the extracted text the lemma counts 59,912 occurrences in
\texttt{clean\_text} and the exact phrase, \emph{board(s) of director(s)} in
\texttt{raw\_text}, 59,749, a difference of 0.3\%. The same entry illustrates
the coupling of \S\ref{sec:lexicon-one} from the other side. Because the phrase
is in the extraction regex, paragraphs that mention it are made hot, and the
zones built around them bring further occurrences of it, and of the governance
text around it, into the extracted text on which it is then counted. It is the
heaviest entry of the scoring vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:lexicon-mass}); part
of that weight is a product of its own role in extraction. The sensitivity
of the index to single entries is reported in \S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon}.

\subsection{The hedge lexicon}
\label{sec:lexicon-hedge}

The \HEDGE\ lexicon is generated by the same script from the Loughran--McDonald
master dictionary \citep{loughran2011}, in the copy distributed with
\texttt{pysentiment2}, the package that computes \SEN. It keeps four
categories, Uncertainty (297 words), Constraining (184), WeakModal (27) and
StrongModal (19), for 495 unique forms (\nolinkurl{metadata/lm_hedge.txt}).
The forms are kept as written, without lemmatisation, because \HEDGE\ is
matched against the raw extracted text (\S\ref{sec:index-components}). The
lemmatised text is stopword-filtered, and the modals that define two of the
four categories (\emph{may}, \emph{might}, \emph{could}, \emph{must},
\emph{will}), together with \emph{always}, \emph{never} and \emph{perhaps}, are
spaCy stopwords, so on lemmatised text they could never be counted.

On the raw extracted text of the 343 reports, tokenised as \HEDGE\ tokenises
it (lowercase alphabetic words, hyphenated compounds kept whole), the lexicon matches 498,477 of 27,252,597 words (1.83 per
100), and 429 of its 495 forms occur at least once. The count is dominated by
a few forms: \emph{risk} (87,687), \emph{risks} (59,412), \emph{will}
(40,489), \emph{may} (38,628) and \emph{requirements} (20,237), the five most
frequent, are 49.4\% of all hits, and \emph{risk} and \emph{risks} alone
outnumber the next three together. \HEDGE\ is therefore to a large extent a
measure of how often a report names risk. The
lexicon also counts the month: an occurrence of \emph{May} capitalised and next
to a number (\emph{31 May}, \emph{May 2023}) is at least 6,632 of the 38,628
hits on \emph{may} (17.2\%) and 1.3\% of all hits. This is a lower bound, as
it misses the month where no number is adjacent; the lexicon is used as
published, without removing these.

\subsection{What the construction does and does not establish}
\label{sec:lexicon-limits}

The construction makes each entry traceable to a source, a criterion, a type,
a pillar and a sectoral flag, and it guarantees that every term is counted in
the same representation as the text it is counted on. It does not validate the
vocabulary, for three reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Built on the evaluation corpus.} The review sample is drawn from
  the 343 reports, and the sense criterion and the dispersion cut were applied
  to occurrences in the same corpus the index is computed on. There is no
  hold-out, and the stability results of \S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon} inherit
  this dependence.
\item \emph{Judgements, not measurements.} Admission, the choice between a
  bare head and its collocations, the pillar and the sectoral designation are
  judgements under stated criteria; the paper reports no error rate for the
  vocabulary's matches, and the concentration thresholds behind the sectoral
  designation are not in a committed script.
\item \emph{Extraction and scoring match differently.} Patterns select text but
  never enter \SUS; the extraction regex and the lemmatised n-grams match
  slightly different strings for the same term
  (\S\ref{sec:extraction-regex}); and an entry's count depends in part on the
  zones it helped select (\S\ref{sec:lexicon-represent}).
\end{enumerate}
The TRANS assignment of the taxonomy family and the sectoral criteria are open
choices; \S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon} reports how much the index depends on
them.
