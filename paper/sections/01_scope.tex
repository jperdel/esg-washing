\section{Introduction and scope}
\label{sec:scope}

This paper measures one quantity: how the tone of a report's ESG disclosure
stands relative to the substance of that disclosure, within a defined corpus.
The corpus is 343 reports of 49 listed firms in seven European countries over
2018--2024 (\S\ref{sec:data}). For each report we compute a tone score, the
Loughran--McDonald polarity of its ESG text \citep{loughran2011}, and a
substance score, the density of mentions of a published ESG vocabulary, and we
combine them as the difference of their standardised values,
$\ESGSI = \Z(\SEN) - \Z(\SUS)$ (\S\ref{sec:index}). A report scores high when
its tone ranks higher in the corpus than its substance does, and low in the
opposite case.

\paragraph{What the score is, and what it is not.} The score is continuous and
corpus-relative. Both components are standardised over the pooled 343 reports,
so a value is a position within this corpus and has no meaning outside it. Its
sign says only on which side of the corpus mean a report falls. We use no
threshold and we classify no report: the score does not separate reports into
ESG-washing and genuine disclosure, and nothing in the paper should be read as
such a classification. The reason is not caution for its own sake. A cut-off
on a standardised difference is a statement about the location of two
distributions, not about firm conduct (\S\ref{sec:artefact}), and no external
benchmark of ESG-washing exists against which a cut-off for these reports could
be calibrated (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). The motivating idea --- positive
language in excess of the substance behind it --- tells us which direction of
the score is of interest. It does not turn the score into a detector.

\paragraph{The two scores.} The paper reports two scores. The first, $\ESGSI$,
takes its architecture and its name from \citet{lagasio2024}: a tone component
minus a substance component, each rescaled across the sample. We keep that
architecture and change how its parts are specified, for reasons given in
\S\ref{sec:justification}. $\ESGSI$ is the main score of the paper. The second,
the Extended ESGSI ($\ESGSIext$), is a new metric that we propose. It adds two
components, both computed on the raw extracted text: $\QUANT$, the density of
quantitative expressions, which counts as further evidence of substance, and
$\HEDGE$, the share of hedging and modal words, which counts on the side of
tone. The two enter with weights of $0.5$. That weighting is our choice and is
not estimated, so we report how far the score moves over a grid of alternative
weights (\S\ref{sec:robustness}).

\paragraph{Relation to the antecedent.} \citet{lagasio2024} introduced the
ESGSI as a cross-section of 749 standalone English-language ESG reports for
2023. We depart from it at six points (Table~\ref{tab:scope-divergence}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Corpus.} The original retains only standalone ESG reports;
        ``companies that integrated their ESG disclosure within their annual
        financial reports were excluded'' \citep[p.~3]{lagasio2024}. Our
        corpus is a balanced panel, and a balanced panel requires one
        disclosure that every firm publishes every year. The annual report,
        and in France the universal registration document (URD), is such a
        filing. A standalone sustainability report is issued at the firm's
        discretion. Of our 49 firms, one is represented by standalone
        sustainability reports, in all seven years; a panel restricted to that
        document type would have held a single firm. The corpus therefore
        consists of 235 annual reports, 101 URD and 7 standalone sustainability
        reports, largely the population the original excludes.
  \item \emph{Extraction.} The original vectorises whole reports. An annual
        report or URD embeds its ESG disclosure in a much larger financial and
        legal document, so we score lexically extracted ESG zones
        (\S\ref{sec:extraction}).
  \item \emph{Substance measure.} The original derives its substance score
        from TF-IDF values and states neither how per-term values are
        aggregated nor whether document vectors are normalised
        (\S\ref{sec:original}). We measure substance as mentions per 100
        tokens. Under L2 vector normalisation, which is the library default, a
        TF-IDF score cannot distinguish a report from the same report with
        twenty times the ESG content, and it tracks the breadth of the
        vocabulary a report uses rather than the amount of disclosure
        (\S\ref{sec:measurement}). Density also agrees more closely with
        independently counted quantitative content, although on fully disjoint
        bases the difference is not significant once firms are resampled
        (\S\ref{sec:validity}).
  \item \emph{Sentiment dictionary.} The original uses TextBlob polarity, a
        general-purpose tool. We use the Loughran--McDonald finance dictionary,
        which was built on disclosure language. The original's own conclusion
        names ``refining sentiment analysis techniques''
        \citep[p.~10]{lagasio2024} as an extension.
  \item \emph{Score normalisation.} The original prints a difference of
        $z$-scores but describes, and on the evidence of its own tables
        computes, a difference of min--max-rescaled scores
        (\S\ref{sec:original}). We use $z$-scores (\S\ref{sec:artefact}).
  \item \emph{No threshold.} The original labels every report ``Potential
        ESGwashing'' if its index exceeds zero and ``Likely Genuine''
        otherwise \citep[pp.~4--5]{lagasio2024}. We do not use this rule or
        any other.
\end{itemize}
Because corpus, vocabulary and sentiment dictionary all differ, our scores and
those of the original are different measurements. No numerical comparison
between them is attempted.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four contributions. First, it
specifies $\ESGSI$ so that each of its parts is fully determined and
justified: a density substance measure, chosen over TF-IDF with L2 vector
normalisation on algebraic grounds and corroborated by a convergent-validity
test with firm-clustered intervals; $z$-score normalisation; and no threshold
(\S\ref{sec:index}). Second, it proposes the Extended ESGSI and reports its
sensitivity to its weights (\S\ref{sec:index-composite},
\S\ref{sec:robustness}). Third, it documents and publishes the ESG vocabulary,
its sources, admission criteria and composition, and the hedge lexicon
(\S\ref{sec:lexicon}, \S\ref{sec:repro}), and it shows how far the score moves
when that vocabulary is perturbed (\S\ref{sec:robustness}). Fourth, it
describes a seven-year balanced panel of European listed firms by year,
industry, country and ESG pillar, with inference that treats the firm, not the
report, as the independent unit (\S\ref{sec:temporal}). The original itself
calls for ``longitudinal studies to examine how changes in corporate
characteristics and regulatory environments affect ESGSI over time''
\citep[p.~10]{lagasio2024}.

\paragraph{Roadmap.} \S\ref{sec:data} describes the corpus,
\S\ref{sec:extraction} the extraction of ESG zones, and \S\ref{sec:lexicon}
the vocabulary and the hedge lexicon. \S\ref{sec:index} defines every
component, both scores and the panel inference, and explains why substance is
measured by density and why the scores are $z$-standardised and not
thresholded. \S\ref{sec:robustness} reports the sensitivity of the scores to
the substance specification, to the weights of the Extended ESGSI and to the
vocabulary. \S\ref{sec:temporal} gives the results by year, industry, country
and pillar, \S\ref{sec:limitations} the limitations, and
Appendix~\ref{sec:repro} the parameters, files and scripts that reproduce every
reported number.
