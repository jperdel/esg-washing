\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section asks how tone relative to substance moved over 2018--2024 and why, how much of the
movement reporting mandates could account for, and where firms, industries and countries stand.
Components are in corpus standard deviations and $\ESGSI$~\eqref{eq:esgsi} and
$\ESGSIext$~\eqref{eq:esgsiext} in standard deviations of their components
(\S\ref{sec:method-choices}), with trends and intervals estimated as in
\S\ref{sec:method-inference}. Industries are ordered by mean $\ESGSI$ throughout.

\subsection{The yearly series and the trend}
\label{sec:results-time}

Both scores fall in every year of the window: mean $\ESGSI$ from $0.557$ in 2018 to $-1.109$ in
2024, mean $\ESGSIext$ from $0.614$ to $-1.094$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-trend},
Figure~\ref{fig:results-time}a). The within-firm trend is $-0.239$ per year for $\ESGSI$ (95\%
interval $[-0.303, -0.176]$) and $-0.255$ for $\ESGSIext$ ($[-0.335, -0.176]$), $-0.189$ and
$-0.167$ standard deviations of each score itself, and both are significant under clustered and
bootstrap inference alike ($p < 10^{-4}$). The decline is not linear. A model with a mean for
every year fits better than the line ($p < 10^{-4}$ for $\ESGSI$, $p < 0.001$ for $\ESGSIext$),
because of a break in 2024, when mean $\ESGSI$ drops by $0.89$ against at most $0.27$ in any
earlier year. Over 2018--2023 the slope is $-0.161$ ($[-0.227, -0.095]$; $\ESGSIext$: $-0.190$),
still clearly negative. The slope over the full window is therefore a summary of a steady decline
followed by a step, and the standard errors do not cover a shock common to all firms in one year
(\S\ref{sec:method-inference}). The crossing of zero between 2021 and 2022 is not an event, since
both scores average zero over the window by construction.

What moves steadily is substance. A lower score can mean less positive tone or more substance
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-time}b). $\susdens$ rises in every year, from $5.75$ to $8.22$ mentions
per 100 tokens ($0.384$ a year, about $0.31$ of its within-firm standard deviation), because
mentions per report grow faster ($+218\%$) than the extracted text that divides them
($+127\%$). Tone has no trend over 2018--2023 (slope $-0.005$, $p = 0.87$); its yearly means stay
within $0.11$ of zero until 2024, when $\Z(\SEN)$ falls to $-0.255$. That fall comes from the
negative list, and from a particular part of it: the words that grow most between 2023 and 2024
are \emph{negative}, \emph{incidents}, \emph{bribery}, \emph{complaints}, \emph{harassment},
\emph{forced}, \emph{corruption}, \emph{violations} and \emph{grievance}, the vocabulary of the
negative impacts, incidents and grievance mechanisms on which the ESRS require firms to report
(\S\ref{sec:results-mandate}). With firm fixed effects the 2024 step of $\Z(\SEN)$ is $-0.30$
($[-0.50, -0.09]$) against 2018--2023; recomputing $\SEN$ without 24 negative words that name
ESRS disclosure topics reduces it to $-0.02$ ($p = 0.87$), and a broader list of 48 such words
gives the same result. Over the full window $\SEN$ has no detectable trend ($p = 0.18$);
a failure to reject is not a demonstrated null. $\ESGSIext$ falls at about the same rate as
$\ESGSI$: $\QUANT$, counted as figures only, and $\HEDGE$ have no trend. In terms of disclosure
practice, the reports devote a growing part of their ESG text to identifiable ESG content without
putting more figures on it, and in 2024 they also report more adverse events: the scores fall
because ESG disclosure became denser and, in the last year, more prescribed, not because firms
came to describe their ESG performance less favourably.

The panel is balanced, so the series is not a change in the set of firms or in the country mix,
and the document-type mix barely moves (\S\ref{sec:data}). Within Germany, fourteen firms with
one country and one document type, both slopes are steeper than the pooled ones: $-0.360$ for
$\ESGSI$ and $-0.412$ for $\ESGSIext$ ($p < 0.001$ under both inferences). A trend of this sign
therefore exists where country and document type do not vary, although this does not show that
their mix plays no part in the pooled slope.

\subsection{Regime vocabulary and the mandate confound}
\label{sec:results-mandate}

Every firm in the panel published a mandatory non-financial statement from 2018 onwards; over the
window its content became increasingly prescribed, by the alignment indicators of the EU
Taxonomy, first published for 2022 by non-financial firms and for 2023 by financial firms, and by
statements under the ESRS for 2024. A firm that adds a taxonomy-alignment table or a standards-referenced sustainability
statement adds ESG vocabulary without necessarily changing anything it does, and $\susdens$
cannot tell the two apart. The vocabulary names regimes that did not exist or were not in force
in 2018, and the transversal pillar that holds them nearly doubles its share of all mentions,
from 10\% to 19\% (\S\ref{sec:results-pillars}).

The purely definitional part can be bounded by deleting entries and rescoring, with the
extraction held fixed. Deleting an explicit list of 22 regime, standard and rating names (3.9\%
of all mentions; deliberately over-inclusive, since several predate the window) leaves an
$\ESGSI$ slope of $-0.207$ ($p < 0.001$), about six sevenths of the original, with a rank
correlation of $0.994$ with the full-vocabulary score. Deleting the whole transversal pillar (30
scoring entries holding 21 of the 22 names and 14.3\% of all mentions) leaves $-0.174$ ($p <
0.001$ under both inferences), about three quarters, with a rank correlation of $0.973$.
$\ESGSIext$ keeps $-0.222$ and $-0.190$ of its $-0.255$. Regime names thus account for about a
seventh of the slope and the transversal pillar for about a quarter; the rest rests largely on
environmental vocabulary that the same mandates also elicit.

Neither check disposes of the mandate confound. A regime elicits substantive disclosure as well as
its own names (emissions, transition plans, most of the environmental vocabulary) in the words a
firm that had changed its conduct would also use, and the environmental pillar is where the rise
sits (\S\ref{sec:results-pillars}). A mandate is a common time shock, collinear with calendar
year; the balanced panel, the German subsample and the fixed effects remove cross-sectional
composition, not anything that moves with time. The mandates offer only partial variation in
exposure. Taxonomy alignment entered one year later for the financial firms, which report on a
different template; we code it by the template each firm actually used, so that
35 firms first report alignment for 2022 and 14 for 2023. The ESRS
were legally required for 2024 only where the corporate sustainability reporting directive had
been transposed, for 22 firms, but 23 of the other 27 also
published an ESRS-referenced statement for that year. With firm and year fixed effects, the
first alignment year shifts $\Z(\susdens)$ by $0.05$ (SE $0.13$) and the legal ESRS requirement by
$-0.07$ (SE $0.19$); the smallest effects these designs could detect (80\,\% power) are
$0.36$ and $0.53$ standard deviations, against a rise of $1.31$ over the window.
The steps in exposure produce no detectable jump of their own, which is consistent with
anticipation, with a drift unrelated to the mandates, or with too little power, and the
confound is left unresolved. Neither step moves $\ESGSI$ detectably either ($-0.09$, SE $0.17$, and
$-0.07$, SE $0.28$). One consequence belongs to the architecture: since both scores fall when
substance rises, a firm that adds a mandated disclosure moves down the scale for having complied.
In 2024 the same holds on the side of tone, since reporting the negative impacts, incidents and
grievances that the ESRS require adds words of the negative list (\S
ef{sec:results-time}). A user of the scores should
therefore read the decline over this window as denser disclosure, whatever its cause, and not as
evidence that firms became more candid.

\subsection{Firms, industries and countries}
\label{sec:results-groups}

Most of what the scores record is a difference between firms. For $\ESGSI$, 59.2\% of the
variation lies between firm means and 16.9\% between year means, and the correlation between two
reports of the same firm, net of year (the intraclass correlation), is $0.669$ ($\ESGSIext$:
61.2\%, 12.4\%, $0.654$). The two components divide this differently: tone varies almost only
between firms (69.7\% of $\Z(\SEN)$ between firm means, 1.2\% between years), while substance
also moves with the year (56.9\% and 18.4\%). Positions persist from one year to the next and
reshuffle over the window: the Spearman correlation of firms' $\ESGSI$ between consecutive years
averages $0.842$ (range $0.770$ to $0.916$) but is $0.303$ between 2018 and 2024 ($\ESGSIext$:
$0.816$ and $0.319$). The decline is general but not uniform: 43 of the 49 firm slopes are negative
for $\ESGSI$ and 45 for $\ESGSIext$.

Industry and country are constant within firm, so every comparison between groups rests on 49
firms in cells of one to seventeen (Table~\ref{tab:results-groups}), and every country contrast
is also a document-type contrast (\S\ref{sec:data}).

\paragraph{Levels.} Industry means of $\ESGSI$ run from $1.085$ (Technology, three firms) to
$-1.872$ (Energy, two); Utilities has by far the highest $\susdens$ and the second-lowest score.
Both ends rest on two or three firms, dispersion within an industry can exceed the distance
between industries (Financials' firm means span $-1.56$ to $1.05$), and the ordering moves within
the window (Figure~\ref{fig:results-industry}): Basic Materials and Telecommunications go from
well above the corpus mean in 2018 to below it in 2024. $\ESGSIext$ reorders the industries where
quantification and hedging differ most: Financials, with the highest mean $\HEDGE$ and among the
lowest $\QUANT$, moves from $-0.254$ to $0.077$, and Utilities, with the highest $\QUANT$, from
$-0.980$ to $-2.600$. Within a single country and document regime, industry orders the firm means
of $\ESGSI$ in France (adjusted $R^2$ of $0.557$; 17 firms, seven industries) but not in Germany
($-0.312$; 14 firms, seven industries).

Germany stands apart among countries ($0.611$ against $-0.056$ for France), and in four of the
five industries present in both countries the German mean exceeds the French one; the gap is a
contrast of country and document type at once, and neither is identified. By document type,
annual reports average $0.023$, URDs $0.021$ and the one firm's sustainability reports
$-1.059$. At report level, year explains $0.169$ of the variance of $\ESGSI$, industry adds
$0.151$ to $0.211$ and country $0.060$ to $0.120$, depending on the order of entry, and firm
identity still adds $0.309$ of $R^2$ after year, industry, country and document type
($\ESGSIext$: $0.236$); across the 49 firm means, industry and country together reach an adjusted
$R^2$ of $0.211$ ($\ESGSIext$: $0.419$). Most of the difference between firms is firm-specific: a
report's position says more about its firm's own reporting practice than about the firm's sector
or country.

\paragraph{Slopes.} Group slopes are read through the intervals on the group's firm-level slopes
(Table~\ref{tab:results-groups}, \S\ref{sec:method-inference}). Energy shows why: its $\ESGSI$
slope has a clustered interval of $[-0.415, -0.176]$ and a firm-slope interval of $[-1.338,
0.748]$. Read this way, the $\ESGSI$ decline is distinguishable from zero in Consumer
Discretionary (10 firms), Consumer Staples (5), Financials (11), Germany (14) and France (17);
Industrials (9) has the flattest decline among the larger industries, with an interval that just
includes zero, and under $\ESGSIext$ it also declines distinguishably. No group of one to three
firms supports a statement about its slope: with two firms the interval rests on one degree of
freedom, so even Utilities, whose two firms both rise, says nothing about the industry.

\subsection{Pillar composition}
\label{sec:results-pillars}

The rise in mentions is not spread evenly across the pillars of \S\ref{sec:method-vocab}
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-pillars}b). The environmental share of all mentions grows from 32\% in
2018 to 42\% in 2024 and the transversal share from 10\% to 19\%, while governance falls from 39\%
to 23\% and the social share stays between 16\% and 20\%. In density, environmental mentions rise
in every year, from $1.73$ to $3.35$ per 100 tokens, and transversal ones from $0.63$ to $1.57$,
while governance mentions fall from $2.34$ to $1.90$: governance loses ground absolutely, not only
by dilution. The rise in substance is, in other words, environmental and regulatory
(\S\ref{sec:results-mandate}).

Across industries the composition follows the business (Figure~\ref{fig:results-pillars}a). The
environmental share runs from 28\% (Financials) to 63\% (Utilities), whose environmental
density of $6.04$ mentions per 100 tokens, against $4.11$ in Energy, the next industry, gives it
the highest $\susdens$ in Table~\ref{tab:results-groups}; Financials and Telecommunications are
the governance-heavy industries (37\% and 35\%). The entries most over-represented in an industry
are its own vocabulary (\emph{hydroelectric} and \emph{smart meter} in Utilities, \emph{oil
spill} and \emph{methane} in Energy), so a firm scores substance partly for describing its
business. The sectoral entries hold 1.0\% of all mentions and at most 2.7\% in any industry
(Consumer Staples), too little to account for the position of Utilities or Energy. Mentions are
concentrated (15 of the 399 scoring entries hold 48.1\% of them); scoring without the sectoral
entries, without each pillar and without the most frequent entry is examined in
\S\ref{sec:robustness}.

\paragraph{Reading the results.} Neither score measures a quantity
(\S\ref{sec:method-choices}): a yearly mean of $-1.109$ is a position within this corpus, and no
level here is comparable with another application of the architecture. What is interpretable is
the ordering within the sample and the direction of change: denser ESG disclosure, at unchanged
tone until the prescribed statements of 2024, and firms differing far more than years. None of this is
evidence about conduct: the reporting mandates of the same window could move the scores with
conduct held fixed, and this design cannot exclude them (\S\ref{sec:results-mandate}).
