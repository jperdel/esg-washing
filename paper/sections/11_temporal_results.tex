\section{Results: time, firms, industries and countries}
\label{sec:temporal}

This section reports both scores on the panel of \S\ref{sec:data}: $\ESGSI$, the main score, and
$\ESGSIext$, the extended score this paper proposes (\S\ref{sec:index-composite}). Both are
continuous and corpus-relative: each component is a population $z$-score over the 343 reports
\eqref{eq:zscore}, so a value is a position relative to the corpus mean, in corpus standard
deviations, whose sign says only on which side of that mean a report lies. No threshold is
applied and no report is classified. Firms enter only as anonymous positions, and firm-level
heterogeneity is reported as distributions by industry.

The specification is the adopted one: $\SUS = \susdens$, our vocabulary with its sectoral
entries ($V = 399$, \S\ref{sec:lexicon}), and $w_q = w_h = 0.5$ in $\ESGSIext$, whose weight
sensitivity is in \S\ref{sec:robustness}. Inference follows \S\ref{sec:index-inference}:
firm-clustered standard errors (49 clusters, $t_{48}$) and, for fixed-effects slopes, a
restricted wild cluster bootstrap; conventional OLS $p$-values appear only beside them,
labelled. Two scores are compared by Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$ and the overlap of their
extreme deciles (the 34 highest and the 34 lowest of 343 reports). Values come from
\nolinkurl{scripts/analysis_v3.py}, figures from \nolinkurl{scripts/figures_v3.py}; industries
are ordered by mean $\ESGSI$ throughout.

Three qualifications attach. The slope summarises an uneven path, not a constant annual rate.
The zero crossing between 2020 and 2021 is not an event: both scores have mean zero over the
window by construction. And a shock common to all firms in one year is not covered, since seven
years cannot be clustered on; the standard error measures dispersion across firms around the
yearly means, not uncertainty about which years were drawn (\S\ref{sec:index-inference}).

Since $\ESGSI = \Z(\SEN) - \Z(\SUS)$, a decline means tone falling or substance rising, and it is
substance (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-series}b, Table~\ref{tab:temporal-drivers}). The first five
quantities of the table rise in every year: mentions outgrow a denominator that more than
doubles, and spread over more of the vocabulary. $\SEN$ has no detectable trend ($+0.0020$ per
year, $p_{\mathrm{cl}} = 0.581$, $p_{\mathrm{wild}} = 0.577$); its yearly means dip in 2020 and
rise in 2024, which works against the decline. A failure to reject is not a demonstrated null,
and it answers one objection only, that a fixed sentiment dictionary scores 2024 language
differently from 2018 language; for the ESG vocabulary, which carries the decline, the
equivalent objection is open (\S\ref{sec:temporal-mandate}).

$\ESGSIext$ subtracts $0.5\,\Z(\QUANT)$ and adds $0.5\,\Z(\HEDGE)$, and its steeper decline comes
from quantification. $\QUANT$ rises by half over the window, not in every year (its standardised
mean peaks in 2023), with a clearly positive slope ($p_{\mathrm{cl}} = 3.4\times10^{-6}$);
$\HEDGE$ is flat ($p_{\mathrm{cl}} = 0.633$), its standardised yearly means within $\pm0.06$. The
\texttt{frameworks} family of $\QUANT$ (Table~\ref{tab:index-quant}) names regimes, so the rise
in $\QUANT$ is exposed to the same chronology objection as $\SUS$. Both scores register words,
not activity.

\subsection{Alternative explanations and the mandate confound}
\label{sec:temporal-mandate}

\paragraph{Composition, length, extraction, country.} The balanced panel excludes drift in the
set of firms and in the country mix by construction, and the document-type mix shifts only
through the six firms that file more than one type (Table~\ref{tab:data-year}); it selects
continuous reporters, which restricts external validity without generating an internal trend. A report that grows without adding ESG
content lowers $\susdens$, so the rise is net of any such dilution. The extraction yield rises in
every year, from 31.6\% to 48.4\% (Table~\ref{tab:extraction-by-year}), but context paragraphs
enter the zones under no density requirement (\S\ref{sec:extraction}), so marginal text should
dilute $\susdens$ rather than inflate it --- an argument, not a measurement. Within Germany
neither country nor document type varies, and the slope is steeper than the pooled one for both
scores: a trend of this sign exists where the confound is absent, which does not show the
confound absent from the pooled slope and says nothing about what acts on all countries at once.

\paragraph{The confound this design cannot remove.} Over 2018--2024 European non-financial
reporting moved from largely voluntary to substantially prescribed. A firm that adds a
taxonomy-alignment table, a framework index and a standards-referenced sustainability statement
adds ESG vocabulary in quantity without necessarily changing anything it does, and our numerator
cannot tell the two apart. The vocabulary names regimes that did not exist or were not in force
in 2018 (\emph{csrd}, \emph{esrs}, the \emph{taxonomy} family, \emph{sfdr}, \emph{issb}), and the
transversal pillar that holds them nearly doubles its share of all mentions, from 9.9\% to
18.6\% (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-pillars}b).

The purely definitional part can be bounded by deleting entries and rescoring, with the
extraction held fixed (Table~\ref{tab:temporal-frameworks}). The first deletion removes an
explicit list of 22 regime, standard and rating names (the \emph{csrd}, \emph{esrs},
\emph{taxonomy}, \emph{sfdr} and \emph{double materiality} family, reporting standards and
initiatives from \emph{global reporting initiative} to \emph{tnfd}, and rating providers),
deliberately over-inclusive (GRI, SASB, CDP and the Global Compact predate the window) so that
it bounds the definitional component from above. The second removes the whole TRANS pillar, 30
entries, which holds 21 of the 22 names (\emph{science based target} is filed under E) and also
\emph{sustainability}, \emph{sustainable} and \emph{esg}.

Striking the regime names leaves an $\ESGSI$ slope of $-0.149$, about four fifths of the
original; striking the whole pillar leaves $-0.117$, about two thirds ($p_{\mathrm{wild}} =
1.0\times10^{-4}$ and $0.003$). $\ESGSIext$ keeps three quarters or more of its slope in both
cases. The trend is not an artefact of counting regime names.

The check does \emph{not} dispose of the mandate confound. A regime elicits substantive
disclosure as well as its own acronyms --- emissions, transition plans, most of the
environmental vocabulary --- in the words a firm that had changed its conduct would also use,
and the environmental pillar is where the rise sits (\S\ref{sec:temporal-pillars}). The
deletions bound the definitional component only; nothing here measures how much of the
remaining slope the mandates produced. Breadth is a weak counterweight: a compliance template
predicts more mentions on few entries, and we observe broadening instead, but the mandates
prescribe reporting across all three pillars at once. A mandate is a common time shock,
collinear with calendar year and outside what firm-clustered inference covers; the balanced
panel, the German subsample and the fixed effects remove cross-sectional composition, not
anything that moves with time. Identification would need variation in exposure (firms outside
scope, staggered application), which 49 large European issuers, all in scope, do not provide.
The confound is therefore left explicitly unresolved.

What the series identifies is that ESG-lexical content per unit of extracted text rose while
tone did not. Whether the added text reflects changed conduct, compliance with a new regime, or
unchanged conduct newly described in a prescribed vocabulary is not identified, and this design
cannot rank the three. One property belongs to the architecture: both scores fall when
substance rises, so a firm that adds a mandated taxonomy table moves down the scale for having
complied, in any use of this index family across a period of regulatory change.

\subsection{Firms}
\label{sec:temporal-firms}

Most of what the scores record is a difference between firms, not between years. For $\ESGSI$,
66.7\% of the total sum of squares lies between firm means and 7.2\% between year means, and the
intraclass correlation of a firm's seven reports, net of year, is $0.676$ ($\ESGSIext$: 68.5\%,
9.4\%, $0.720$). The standard deviation of the 49 firm means of $\ESGSI$ is $1.123$, against a
mean within-firm standard deviation of $0.753$; a firm's yearly values span $2.09$ on average
($2.53$ for $\ESGSIext$), more than the distance between the 2018 and 2024 yearly means. The
trend is real in the sense of Table~\ref{tab:temporal-trend}, and small beside this.

Positions persist. The Spearman correlation of firms' $\ESGSI$ between consecutive years runs
from $0.728$ (2023--2024) to $0.915$ (2021--2022), mean $0.849$, but is $0.456$ between 2018 and
2024; for $\ESGSIext$, $0.672$ to $0.933$ (mean $0.823$) and $0.393$. Ranks are stable from one
year to the next and reshuffle substantially over the window. Across firms the two scores agree
about as well as across reports: firm means correlate $r = 0.887$ ($\rho = 0.848$), firm slopes
$r = 0.876$ ($\rho = 0.860$).

The decline is general but not uniform: 39 of the 49 firm slopes are negative for $\ESGSI$ and
43 for $\ESGSIext$, with medians $-0.177$ and $-0.272$ (their means are the fixed-effects
slopes); the standard deviation of the $\ESGSI$ firm slopes is $0.242$.
Table~\ref{tab:temporal-firms} and Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-firms} give the distribution by
industry. Dispersion within an industry is often as large as the distance between industries:
Financials (11 firms) spans $-1.41$ to $2.09$ in firm means of $\ESGSI$, Consumer Discretionary
(10) $-1.93$ to $1.61$. Utilities, two firms, is the only industry with no negative firm slope,
under either score. Nothing in this subsection concerns a firm's conduct.

\subsection{Industries and countries}
\label{sec:temporal-sectors}

not the 343 reports, in cells of 1 to 17 firms. Industry cuts across countries and is partly
informative about the country/document-type confound (\S\ref{sec:data-industry}); country is
not. The document types are almost perfectly sorted by country (99 of the 101 URDs are French,
all 98 German reports are annual reports, the seven sustainability reports are one French
firm's), so every country contrast is also a document-type contrast.

\paragraph{Levels.} Industry means of $\ESGSI$ run from $1.125$ (Technology, 3 firms) to
$-1.919$ (Energy, 2 firms); Utilities has the highest mean $\susdens$, $9.86$, and the
second-lowest score. Both ends rest on two or three firms, and the ordering moves within the
window (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-industry-year}): Basic Materials and Telecommunications go from
well above the corpus mean in 2018 to below it in 2024. $\ESGSIext$ reorders the industries where
quantification and hedging differ most: Financials, with the highest mean $\HEDGE$ and among the
lowest $\QUANT$, moves from $0.041$ to $0.791$; Utilities, with the highest $\QUANT$, from
$-1.171$ to $-2.573$. Industry therefore structures $\ESGSIext$ more than $\ESGSI$
(Table~\ref{tab:temporal-variance}).

Among countries, Germany stands apart (mean $\ESGSI$ $1.003$ against $-0.335$ for France), but
its reports are all annual reports and France's mostly URDs, so the gap is not a country effect
we can identify. By document type, annual reports average $0.167$, URDs $-0.263$ and the seven
sustainability reports, one firm's, $-1.792$: no document-type effect is identified either.
Within France, industry accounts for an adjusted $R^2$ of $0.191$ of the variance of firm means
(17 firms, seven industries); within Germany, $-0.464$ (14 firms, seven industries). Neither
supports an industry ordering of $\ESGSI$. In each of the five industries present in both
countries the German mean exceeds the French one, so the country gap survives holding industry
fixed, and so does the document-type confound it carries.

\paragraph{Slopes.} Group slopes are read through the firm-slope intervals of
Table~\ref{tab:temporal-groups}: the clustered interval of a group slope rests on $M_k - 1$ free
contributions but is referred to $t_{48}$, and is too narrow for small groups
(\S\ref{sec:index-inference}). Utilities shows the difference: its positive $\ESGSI$ slope has a
clustered interval of $[0.143, 0.421]$ and a firm-slope interval of $[-0.933, 1.497]$. Read this
way, the $\ESGSI$ slope is distinguishable from zero in Consumer Discretionary (10 firms),
Consumer Staples (5), Germany (14) and France (17). Financials (11) and Industrials (9) have the
flattest declines among the larger industries, with intervals that just include zero; Italy and
Spain are nearly flat. Under $\ESGSIext$, whose slopes are steeper, Financials, Industrials and
the Netherlands also decline distinguishably. No group of one to three firms, including both
industries with positive slopes, has an $\ESGSI$ interval narrow enough to say anything; the
$\ESGSIext$ exceptions (Energy, Finland) are two-firm intervals on one degree of freedom.

\subsection{Pillar composition and the mass of the vocabulary}
\label{sec:temporal-pillars}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_pillars.pdf}
\caption{Share of ESG mentions by pillar, pooled over the group's reports
\eqref{eq:pillar-share}, (a) by ICB industry, ordered by mean $\ESGSI$ as in
Table~\ref{tab:temporal-groups}, and (b) by year.}
\label{fig:temporal-pillars}
\end{figure}

The mentions that $\susdens$ pools do not grow evenly across pillars (\S\ref{sec:index-pillars},
Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-pillars}b): the rise is environmental and transversal, and governance
loses ground. In mean pillar density per 100 tokens \eqref{eq:pillar-density}, the environmental
pillar rises in every year, from $1.73$ to $3.25$; the transversal one more than doubles, from
$0.63$ to $1.52$, though not in every year; the social one is roughly level until 2024 ($1.05$
to $1.38$); governance \emph{falls}, from $2.34$ to $1.90$. These are descriptive means; we
estimate no pillar-level trend regressions. Across industries the composition follows the
business (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-pillars}a): the environmental share runs from 26.2\%
(Telecommunications) to 63.2\% (Utilities), whose environmental density of $6.04$, against
$2.33$ over the corpus, gives it the highest $\susdens$ in Table~\ref{tab:temporal-groups};
Financials and Telecommunications are the governance-heavy industries.

The mass is concentrated: 15 of the 399 entries hold 48.1\% of the 1,047,951 mentions, each
occurring in all 49 firms. The heaviest are \emph{board director} (5.72\%), \emph{remuneration}
(5.65\%) and \emph{emission} (5.09\%), and three of the five heaviest are governance entries, so
any statement about the governance pillar inherits the single-entry sensitivity reported in
\S\ref{sec:robustness}; we make none beyond the descriptive one above.

Distinctive entries by industry are sector vocabulary. Ranked by lift (the entry's share of the
industry's mentions over its share of all mentions, among entries with at least 50 mentions in
the industry and present in two or more of its firms), they include \emph{hydroelectric} (lift
19.1) and \emph{smart meter} (17.3) for Utilities, \emph{oil spill} (12.2) and \emph{methane}
(8.9) for Energy, \emph{sedex} and \emph{food safety} (8.4) for Consumer Staples, and
\emph{access healthcare} (14.7) for Health Care; Financials' are governance terms (\emph{line
defence}, \emph{esg risk management}). A firm thus scores substance partly for describing its
own industry. The sectoral designation of \S\ref{sec:lexicon} covers some of these entries
(\emph{smart meter}, \emph{oil spill}, \emph{sedex}, \emph{food safety}, \emph{access
healthcare}) and not others (\emph{hydroelectric}, \emph{methane}). The 34 sectoral entries hold
1.0\% of all mentions, at most 2.7\% in any industry (Consumer Staples) and 1.7\% in Utilities,
so they cannot account for the distance between Utilities or Energy and the rest; environmental
density at large carries it. Scoring without them is a sensitivity reported in
\S\ref{sec:robustness}.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the series}
\label{sec:temporal-vocabulary}

\paragraph{Substance specification.} $\ESGSI(\sustfidf)$ is nearly identical to the adopted
score (Table~\ref{tab:robustness-index}) and falls in every year
(Table~\ref{tab:temporal-series}); we ran no clustered trend test on it. $\ESGSI(\suslag)$
differs more and declines at $-0.143$ per year (\S\ref{sec:robustness-spec}), but its yearly
mean is nearly flat after 2022. The direction is common to all three
specifications, its significance tested for two, and monotonicity holds for $\susdens$ and
$\sustfidf$ only, as expected since $\suslag$ tracks $\BREADTH$ (\S\ref{sec:robustness}).

\paragraph{Weights and vocabulary.} The weights of $\ESGSIext$ are a choice, not an estimate;
\S\ref{sec:robustness} varies them over a grid and perturbs the vocabulary
(\S\ref{sec:robustness-lexicon}). Neither check bears on the mandate confound, which concerns
chronology, not our discretion over the list; no perturbation of a list built on these documents
(\S\ref{sec:lexicon-limits}) moves the bound of Table~\ref{tab:temporal-frameworks}.

\subsection{What the results license}

Every component is score-normalised across the sample, so neither score has cardinal meaning. A
yearly mean of $-0.610$, or a firm mean of $2.947$, is a position within this corpus, not a
quantity of anything; no level here is comparable with \citet{lagasio2024} or any other
application of the architecture, and slopes are in pooled standard deviations of this sample.
The sign of a score says only on which side of the corpus mean a report lies. Only orderings
within the sample and the direction of change are interpretable.

Within those bounds, both scores decline in every year from 2018 to 2024, at $-0.181$
($\ESGSI$) and $-0.260$ ($\ESGSIext$) corpus standard deviations per year. The decline is carried
by substance, and in $\ESGSIext$ also by quantification; tone and hedging show no detectable
movement. It is not a composition artefact, appears where the country/document-type confound is
absent, holds in direction under all three substance specifications, and survives, at four
fifths and two thirds of its magnitude, the removal of regime names and of the whole transversal
pillar. It is the smaller part of what the scores record: firms differ far more than years,
their positions persist from year to year, and neither industry nor country, on cells this
small, accounts for most of the difference between firms. None of this is evidence about
conduct: the reporting mandates of the same window could move the scores as they move with
conduct held fixed, and this design cannot exclude them (\S\ref{sec:temporal-mandate}).
