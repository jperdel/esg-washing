\section{Specification of the ESGSI and the Extended ESGSI}
\label{sec:index}

This section defines every quantity the paper computes and then explains the
three choices on which the specification rests: substance measured by density
rather than by TF-IDF with vector normalisation, standardisation by $z$-scores,
and no threshold (\S\ref{sec:justification}). Later sections test sensitivity
to these choices (\S\ref{sec:robustness}) and report the resulting scores
(\S\ref{sec:temporal}); none of them redefines anything.

\subsection{Notation and the two text representations}
\label{sec:index-notation}

Let $d = 1,\dots,n$ index the $n = 343$ reports of \S\ref{sec:data} and $j = 1,\dots,V$
the entries of the lemmatised ESG vocabulary of \S\ref{sec:lexicon}, with
$V = 399$: the 365 lemmas of the base terms and the 34 further lemmas
contributed by the sectoral terms (Table~\ref{tab:lexicon-lemma}). The union
removes any sectoral lemma already among the base lemmas; here none is. The 32
regular-expression patterns of the vocabulary act at extraction only and
are not among the $V$ entries. Preprocessing emits two representations of each
document, and the components are not all computed on the same one:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{lemmatised text} --- lowercased, stopword-filtered,
        punctuation- and numeral-stripped, lemmatised (\S\ref{sec:lexicon});
        whitespace token count $\ell_d$, with $L_d = \max(\ell_d, 1)$;
  \item \emph{raw extracted text} --- the ESG zones of \S\ref{sec:extraction}
        with NUL characters dropped, whitespace collapsed and nothing else
        removed, digits and stopwords intact; whitespace token count $r_d$, with
        $R_d = \max(r_d, 1)$.
\end{itemize}

$\QUANT$ and $\HEDGE$ are computed on the raw extracted text; $\SUS$, $\SEN$
and $\BREADTH$ are computed on the lemmatised text. The split is not
incidental. Preprocessing discards numeric tokens, so on lemmatised text the
\texttt{percentages} and \texttt{large\_numbers} families of $\QUANT$ match
nothing and return exactly zero for every document, without error; it also
discards stopwords, among them the modal verbs that $\HEDGE$ counts. Every
length-normalised component is divided by a token count of the
representation it is computed on --- $R_d$ for $\QUANT$, a word count $W_d$
of the same raw text for $\HEDGE$ (below), $L_d$ for the others --- and the
counts are not interchangeable.

Let $c_{dj}$ be the raw count of vocabulary entry $j$ in the lemmatised text
of $d$, obtained with a fixed-vocabulary count vectoriser (scikit-learn's
default tokeniser, lowercased) over token sequences of length $1$ to
$n_{\max}$, where $n_{\max}$ is the token length of the
longest entry, derived from the vocabulary at run time; here
$n_{\max} = 4$ (\emph{supply chain human right}). Entries are counted
independently, so an occurrence of a multi-word entry also counts for any
shorter vocabulary entry it contains. Let $df_j = |\{d : c_{dj} > 0\}|$ and

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:idf}
\mathrm{idf}_j \;=\; \ln\!\frac{1+n}{1+df_j} \;+\; 1 ,
\end{equation}

scikit-learn's smoothed inverse document frequency \citep{pedregosa2011}.
Supplying the vocabulary in advance disables document-frequency and
feature-count filtering, so the \texttt{max\_df}, \texttt{min\_df} and
\texttt{max\_features} settings the original discloses (\S\ref{sec:original})
are inoperative here: all $V$ entries are retained and scored in every
document.
Cross-sectional rescaling of a finished component score $x$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zscore}
\Z(x)_d \;=\; \frac{x_d - \bar{x}}{\sigma_x},
\qquad \sigma_x = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_d (x_d-\bar{x})^2},
\end{equation}
with $\Z(x) \equiv 0$ when $\sigma_x = 0$. Both $\bar{x}$ and $\sigma_x$ are
taken over the $n$ documents analysed, pooled across the seven years, so every
score below is a position within this corpus, not an absolute quantity, and
yearly means of a score are comparable with one another.

\subsection{The substance component: three specifications}
\label{sec:index-sus}

We compute three substance specifications, differing in term weighting and in
vector normalisation. The first is the one $\ESGSI$ uses; the other two are
TF-IDF constructions, reported as alternatives because the antecedent derives
its substance score from TF-IDF without determining which construction
(\S\ref{sec:original}):

\begin{align}
\label{eq:susdens}
\susdens_d   &= 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{j} c_{dj}}{L_d}, \\[4pt]
\label{eq:sustfidf}
\sustfidf_d  &= 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{j} c_{dj}\,\mathrm{idf}_j}{L_d}, \\[4pt]
\label{eq:suslag}
\suslag_d    &= \frac{1}{V} \sum_{j}
                \frac{c_{dj}\,\mathrm{idf}_j}
                     {\bigl\lVert \mathbf{c}_d \odot \mathbf{idf} \bigr\rVert_2},
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{c}_d \odot \mathbf{idf}$ is the elementwise product of the
count vector with the idf vector, and $\suslag_d = 0$ by convention when a
document contains no vocabulary entry. The subscripts name the scaling
applied to the term vector: $\ell$ for division by document length, $L2$ for
vector normalisation, that is scaling the term vector to unit $L_2$ norm.
Equation~\eqref{eq:susdens} is ESG mentions
per 100 lemmatised tokens under uniform term weights.
Equation~\eqref{eq:sustfidf} applies idf weights to the same
length-normalised construction. Equation~\eqref{eq:suslag} is the closest
reading of the antecedent that its description allows: idf weights, L2
vector normalisation of the document's term vector (the library default),
mean over the vocabulary. The factors $100$ and $1/V$ are positive
affine rescalings and are annihilated by \eqref{eq:zscore}; the sum-versus-mean
choice in \eqref{eq:suslag} is therefore immaterial, and the specifications
differ only in weighting and in vector normalisation. Both idf specifications
estimate $df_j$ on the analysed sample and are corpus-dependent;
\eqref{eq:susdens} is not. We adopt $\susdens$ as the substance component of
both scores; \S\ref{sec:justification} gives the reasons and
\S\ref{sec:robustness} the consequences of the alternatives.

\subsection{Tone, quantification, hedging and breadth}
\label{sec:index-components}

\paragraph{Sentiment.} $\SEN$ is Loughran--McDonald polarity
\citep{loughran2011} as implemented in \texttt{pysentiment2}. Its tokeniser
splits the lowercased lemmatised text into maximal runs of letters and
reduces each to its NLTK Porter stem; the dictionary words are stemmed the
same way, giving 140 positive and 893 negative stems, disjoint. Let
$s^{+}_d$ and $s^{-}_d$ count the tokens of $d$ whose stem is positive and
negative respectively:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sen}
\SEN_d \;=\; \frac{s^{+}_d - s^{-}_d}{s^{+}_d + s^{-}_d + \varepsilon},
\qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-6}.
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon$ is the package's constant and only prevents division by
zero. This is a bounded ratio on
$[-1,1]$, not a density: up to $\varepsilon$ it depends only on the balance of
$s^{+}_d$ and $s^{-}_d$, not on how many sentiment-bearing tokens the document
contains, and it is $0$ when there are none.

\paragraph{Quantification.} $\QUANT$ is the density of regular-expression
hits from four families (Table~\ref{tab:index-quant}) over the \emph{raw}
extracted text, matched case-insensitively:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:quant}
\QUANT_d \;=\; \frac{1}{R_d}\sum_{g \in G} m_g(\mathrm{raw}_d),
\end{equation}
with $G$ the four families and $m_g$ the number of non-overlapping matches
of family $g$; a string matched by two families counts twice.

\begin{table}[tbp]
\centering
\caption{The four $\QUANT$ regular-expression families.}
\label{tab:index-quant}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Family & Pattern \\
\midrule
percentages    & \verb@\b\d+(?:[.,]\d+)?\s*%@ \\
large\_numbers & \verb@\b(?!(?:19|20)\d{2}\b)\d{4,}\b@ \\
units          & \verb@\b(?:tonne|ton|mt|ktco2|co2e?|ghg|kwh|mwh|@ \\
               & \verb@gwh|twh|mw|gw|litre|liter|m3|cubic meter)\b@ \\
frameworks     & \verb@\b(?:gri|tcfd|sasb|issb|sdg|ungc|sfdr|csrd|@ \\
               & \verb@un global compact|paris agreement|taxonomy)\b@ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The \texttt{large\_numbers} family excludes four-digit strings of the form
\texttt{19xx} and \texttt{20xx}, so that citing dates does not register as
quantification.

\paragraph{Hedging.} $\HEDGE$ is the share of words of the raw extracted
text that belong to the hedge lexicon $H$ of \S\ref{sec:lexicon}
($|H| = 495$ forms: the Uncertainty, WeakModal, StrongModal and
Constraining words of the master dictionary of \citealp{loughran2011}, as written).
The raw text is lowercased and split into words, the maximal runs of letters
with internal hyphens allowed (\texttt{[a-z]+(?:-[a-z]+)*}), so digits and
punctuation separate words and \emph{ill-defined} is one word. With
$\mathrm{words}(\mathrm{raw}_d)$ that sequence and
$W_d = \max(|\mathrm{words}(\mathrm{raw}_d)|, 1)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hedge}
\HEDGE_d \;=\; \frac{\bigl|\{t \in \mathrm{words}(\mathrm{raw}_d) : t \in H\}\bigr|}{W_d}.
\end{equation}
On the lemmatised text, which is stopword-filtered, the modals \emph{may},
\emph{might}, \emph{could}, \emph{must} and \emph{will}, and \emph{always},
\emph{never} and \emph{perhaps}, could never match; on the raw text every
form of $H$ can. Matching is by form, not by sense: the count is dominated by
\emph{risk} and \emph{risks}, so in these reports $\HEDGE$ largely registers
how often risk is discussed, and \emph{May} the month also counts as a modal
(\S\ref{sec:lexicon-hedge} gives the counts). $\HEDGE$ enters only
$\ESGSIext$.

\paragraph{Breadth.} $\BREADTH$ is the effective number of distinct ESG terms
in a document: the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the term-share
distribution, i.e.\ a Hill number of order one \citep{hill1973}. With
$p_{dj} = c_{dj} / \max\bigl(\sum_{k} c_{dk},\, 1\bigr)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:breadth}
\BREADTH_d \;=\; \exp\Bigl(-\!\!\sum_{j:\,p_{dj}>0} p_{dj} \ln p_{dj}\Bigr)
\;\in\; [1, V].
\end{equation}
It equals $1$ when all mentions fall on one term --- and, by the
$\max(\cdot,1)$ convention in $p_{dj}$, also when a document contains no
vocabulary entry at all --- and $V$ when mentions are spread uniformly across
the vocabulary. \textbf{$\BREADTH$ is not a component of
either score.} It is reported alongside them because it is the quantity
$\suslag$ turns out to measure (\S\ref{sec:measurement}); reporting it as a
separate column is what allows that claim to be checked against the published
results rather than taken on assertion.

\subsection{The two scores}
\label{sec:index-composite}

\begin{align}
\label{eq:esgsi}
\ESGSI_d    &= \Z(\SEN)_d - \Z(\susdens)_d, \\[2pt]
\label{eq:esgsiext}
\ESGSIext_d &= \Z(\SEN)_d - \Z(\susdens)_d - w_q\,\Z(\QUANT)_d + w_h\,\Z(\HEDGE)_d,
\qquad w_q = w_h = 0.5 .
\end{align}

\paragraph{ESGSI.} Equation~\eqref{eq:esgsi} is the main score of the paper.
It keeps the architecture of \citet{lagasio2024} --- a tone component minus a
substance component, each rescaled across the sample --- and reproduces the
formula printed there \citep[p.~4]{lagasio2024}, with our substance and tone
components in place of the original's. Where the substance specification is
varied (\S\ref{sec:robustness}) we write $\ESGSI(\sustfidf)$ and
$\ESGSI(\suslag)$ for the score with \eqref{eq:sustfidf} or
\eqref{eq:suslag} in place of $\susdens$; unqualified, $\ESGSI$ always means
$\ESGSI(\susdens)$.

\paragraph{Extended ESGSI.} Equation~\eqref{eq:esgsiext} is a new metric that
this paper proposes. It adds to $\ESGSI$ two components that the tone and
substance scores do not capture. Quantified disclosure --- percentages,
absolute figures, physical units, named reporting frameworks --- is further
evidence of substance, so $\Z(\QUANT)$ is subtracted, on the same side as
$\Z(\susdens)$. Hedging and modal language qualifies whatever a report
asserts, so $\Z(\HEDGE)$ is added, on the side of tone. Both directions are
part of the proposal rather than findings: $\HEDGE$ counts word forms, and in
these reports it largely registers how often risk is discussed
(\S\ref{sec:index-components}). The weights of $0.5$ give each added
component half the weight of each original one, so that together they weigh
as much as one of the two original components and the tone--substance
contrast remains the core of the score. They are a stated choice, not an
estimate: no external criterion is available against which to calibrate them
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}). \S\ref{sec:robustness-weights} therefore reports
the score over the grid $w_q, w_h \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$, whose corner
$w_q = w_h = 0$ is $\ESGSI$ itself: the ranking depends mostly on $w_h$, the
within-firm trend of \S\ref{sec:index-inference} almost only on $w_q$. The two
scores are related but not interchangeable (\S\ref{sec:temporal}).

\paragraph{Reading the scores.} Both scores are continuous. Every term is a
$z$-score over the pooled corpus, so each score has mean zero by construction,
and its sign says only whether a report lies above or below the corpus mean
of the combination. No threshold is applied and no report is assigned to a
class. \S\ref{sec:artefact} explains why. The comparisons we report --- across
years, industries, countries and specifications --- are comparisons of positions
within the corpus: differences of means, trends, rank correlations and the
overlap of the top and bottom deciles.

\subsection{Pillar decomposition}
\label{sec:index-pillars}

The results decompose $\susdens$ by pillar. The decomposition is descriptive:
it is not a component of either score, it enters neither \eqref{eq:esgsi}
nor \eqref{eq:esgsiext}, and it changes no score. Let $\pi(j) \in
\{\mathrm{E}, \mathrm{S}, \mathrm{G}, \mathrm{TRANS}\}$ be the pillar of entry
$j$ (\S\ref{sec:lexicon}). An entry takes the pillar of the natural-form term
that lemmatises to it; where several terms share one lemma the first in
\texttt{ESG\_terms\_v3.csv} is used, and no two such terms carry different pillars.
The map partitions the $V = 399$ entries into E 152, S 127, G 90 and TRANS 30.
These are not the counts of Table~\ref{tab:lexicon-blocks} (E 168, S 139,
G 95, TRANS 32), which are over the 434 source entries: patterns are not
among the $V$ entries, and some terms collide at lemmatisation. For pillar $p$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pillar-density}
\mathrm{dens}^{p}_d \;=\; 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{j:\,\pi(j)=p} c_{dj}}{L_d},
\qquad \sum_{p} \mathrm{dens}^{p}_d \;=\; \susdens_d ,
\end{equation}
is the number of mentions of pillar-$p$ entries per 100 lemmatised tokens.
For a set $A$ of documents (a year, an industry, a country, or the corpus),
the pillar share of mentions pools counts over the set,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pillar-share}
s^{p}_A \;=\; \frac{\sum_{d \in A} \sum_{j:\,\pi(j)=p} c_{dj}}
                   {\sum_{d \in A} \sum_{j} c_{dj}},
\end{equation}
so that a document weighs in proportion to its number of mentions, while the
pillar density of the set is the unweighted mean of
$\mathrm{dens}^{p}_d$ over $d \in A$. The same two quantities are computed
for the 34 entries contributed only by sectoral terms, a set that cuts across
the pillars. Because entries are counted independently (\S\ref{sec:index-notation}),
the shares partition vocabulary hits, not text: one occurrence of a
multi-word entry adds to every contained entry, possibly in different
pillars.

\subsection{Inference on the panel}
\label{sec:index-inference}

The regressions of \S\ref{sec:temporal} are estimated on the panel of
\S\ref{sec:data}. Write $d = (i, t)$ for the report of firm $i = 1, \dots, M$
in year $t$, with $M = 49$, $N = n = 343$ and $\tau_t$ the calendar year. The
panel is balanced, so every firm is observed in all seven years.
For an outcome $y$ (a score, a component or a density), the pooled
regression is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trend-pooled}
y_{it} \;=\; \alpha + \beta\,\tau_t + u_{it}.
\end{equation}
The firm fixed-effects regression removes firm means,
$\ddot y_{it} = y_{it} - \bar y_{i}$ and
$\ddot\tau_{it} = \tau_t - \bar\tau_i$, and estimates
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trend-fe}
\ddot y_{it} \;=\; \beta\,\ddot\tau_{it} + \ddot u_{it}
\end{equation}
by least squares. In the balanced panel $\bar\tau_i = 2021$ for every firm,
and $\hat\beta_{\mathrm{FE}}$ equals the mean of the 49 firm-level OLS slopes.

Every panel regression is reported with standard errors clustered by firm.
For a regressor matrix $X$ with $K$ columns and residuals $\hat u$, the
variance is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cluster-vcov}
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta) \;=\; \frac{M}{M-1}\,\frac{N-1}{N-K}\,
(X^{\top}X)^{-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{M} X_i^{\top}\hat u_i \hat u_i^{\top} X_i\Bigr)
(X^{\top}X)^{-1},
\end{equation}
with $X_i$ and $\hat u_i$ the rows of firm $i$ (CR1), and $t$ statistics
are referred to a $t$ distribution with $M-1$ degrees of freedom (48 on the
full panel, 13 on the German subsample). In the fixed-effects regression
\eqref{eq:trend-fe} the firm means are removed before estimation, so $K$
counts the slope only. The conventional (homoskedastic, independent) OLS
standard error of \eqref{eq:trend-pooled} is shown alongside where it is
reported at all, and is labelled as such: seven reports of one firm are not
independent observations. Clustering by firm allows any correlation among a
firm's seven reports but assumes independence across firms within a year. A
shock common to all firms in one year, such as a new reporting mandate, is
not covered, and with seven years it cannot be clustered on. The slope
therefore summarises the seven yearly means, and its standard error
reflects dispersion across firms around them, not uncertainty about which
years were drawn.

With $M = 49$ clusters, and with fewer in the subsamples, the asymptotic cluster
approximation can mislead, so the fixed-effects slope is also tested with a
restricted wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008}. Under
$H_0\!: \beta = 0$ the restricted model of \eqref{eq:trend-fe} contains only
the firm effects, so its residuals are $\ddot y_{it}$ itself. For each
replication $b = 1, \dots, B$ we draw one Rademacher weight per firm,
$v^{*}_{ib} \in \{-1, +1\}$ with equal probability, form
$\ddot y^{*}_{itb} = v^{*}_{ib}\,\ddot y_{it}$, re-estimate
\eqref{eq:trend-fe} and compute its cluster-robust $t^{*}_b$. The $p$-value is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wild-p}
p_{\mathrm{wild}} \;=\; \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\lvert t^{*}_b\rvert \geq \lvert \hat t\,\rvert\bigr\},
\end{equation}
with $\hat t$ the observed statistic. The small-sample factor of
\eqref{eq:cluster-vcov} multiplies $\hat t$ and every $t^{*}_b$ alike and
is omitted. We use $B = 9{,}999$ and seed $20260925$ (NumPy
\texttt{default\_rng}); when no $t^{*}_b$ reaches $\lvert\hat t\rvert$ we
report $p_{\mathrm{wild}} < 1/(B+1) = 0.0001$.

Group-specific slopes interact the detrended year with indicators of a
time-invariant firm attribute $\gamma(i)$ (industry, supersector or country),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trend-group}
\ddot y_{it} \;=\; \sum_{k} \beta_k\,\mathbf{1}\{\gamma(i) = k\}\,\ddot\tau_{it}
+ \ddot u_{it},
\end{equation}
with firm-clustered standard errors as in \eqref{eq:cluster-vcov}. In the
balanced panel $\hat\beta_k$ is the mean of the firm-level OLS slopes of the
$M_k$ firms in group $k$, and we also report the $t$ interval on those firm
slopes, with $M_k - 1$ degrees of freedom. The two intervals differ in
their effective degrees of freedom. The normal equation for $\beta_k$ makes
the $M_k$ firm scores of group $k$ sum to zero, so the clustered variance of
$\hat\beta_k$ rests on $M_k - 1$ free contributions. Up to the CR1 factor it
is $(M_k-1)/M_k$ times the squared standard error of the firm-slope mean,
yet it is referred to $t_{M-1}$. For $M_k = 1$ it is exactly zero; for
$M_k = 2$ the firm-slope interval has one degree of freedom
(\S\ref{sec:data-industry}). We therefore read group slopes through the
firm-slope interval and treat the clustered one as too narrow for small
groups. Equality of the $\beta_k$ is tested by a Wald $F$ test on the
clustered variance (denominator degrees of freedom $M-1$). It treats the
slope of a one-firm group as known without error, and is therefore
anti-conservative whenever such a group exists (Belgium among countries, two
supersectors). With two such groups the variance of the restrictions is also
singular, and the test uses its rank. Document type is not used as a grouping because it varies within
six firms. Yearly means are
reported with 95\% intervals from a regression of $y$ on a full set of year
indicators, without constant, again clustered by firm.

\subsection{Why density, \texorpdfstring{$z$}{z}-scores and no threshold}
\label{sec:justification}

Three choices distinguish our specification from its antecedent: substance is
measured by density rather than by TF-IDF with vector normalisation, the
components are standardised by $z$-scores rather than rescaled by min--max,
and no threshold is applied. This subsection gives the reasons. It begins with
what \citet{lagasio2024} specifies, because the first choice answers a gap in
that specification rather than a stated alternative.

\subsubsection{What the antecedent discloses and leaves unstated}
\label{sec:original}

\citet{lagasio2024} computes its sentiment component with TextBlob polarity
and its substance component with scikit-learn's \texttt{TfidfVectorizer},
version 0.24.2, over an author-constructed keyword list ``based on
established ESG reporting frameworks such as Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)
Standards \ldots\ and Sustainable Accounting Standards Board (SASB) Standards''
(pp.~3--4). Four vectoriser settings are disclosed in prose --- terms in more
than 95\,\% of documents ignored, terms in fewer than two ignored, the
vocabulary limited to 10{,}000 terms, unigrams and bigrams --- that is,
\texttt{max\_df=0.95}, \texttt{min\_df=2}, \texttt{max\_features=10000} and
\texttt{ngram\_range=(1,2)} (p.~4). The index is labelled definitionally: a
positive value is ``Potential ESGwashing'', a non-positive one ``Likely
Genuine'' (pp.~4--5).

Four things are not stated. The article never gives the \texttt{norm}
setting, and never describes a rescaling of document vectors under any
wording. It never states how per-term TF-IDF values become a document-level
$\SUS_i$, which is glossed only as ``sustainability score for company i''
(p.~4). It never publishes the keyword list or gives its size; three example
terms appear. And it never says whether the raw or the preprocessed text
reached TextBlob. The Cohen's Kappa, five-fold cross-validation and TF-IDF
sensitivity analyses announced on p.~5 are not reported, and the data
availability statement reads ``No'' (p.~10). One further point is stated
twice, inconsistently: the article prints the index as a difference of
$z$-scores and says in the same subsection that ``both sentiment and
sustainability scores are normalized to a uniform scale using min-max
normalization'' (p.~4).

Of these, the vector normalisation can be supplied by convention. The four
disclosed settings are exactly four non-default arguments, so a reader who
assumes that only departures from library defaults are reported would infer
that \texttt{norm='l2'}, the default, was in force. We make that inference,
and label it as one: $\suslag$ in \eqref{eq:suslag} is the construction it
implies. The aggregation rule has no default to fall back on, but on a
$z$-standardised score it is immaterial, since a sum and a mean over the
vocabulary differ by the factor $1/V$ (\S\ref{sec:index-sus}). What matters,
then, is the vector normalisation, and the next subsection shows why.

\subsubsection{Vector normalisation discards the amount of disclosure}
\label{sec:measurement}

L2 vector normalisation scales a document's term vector to unit length. For
any $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ and scalar $c > 0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:l2-invariance}
\frac{c\,\mathbf{v}}{\lVert c\,\mathbf{v}\rVert_2}
= \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{v}\rVert_2},
\end{equation}
so two documents whose ESG term counts are proportional receive identical
unit vectors, and any aggregation of those vectors returns identical scores.
The normalised vector keeps the mix of terms and discards how much there is.
That suits retrieval, where TF-IDF originated
\citep{sparckjones1972,manning2008} and a document's scale is a nuisance. It
does not suit a score whose purpose is to set tone against the amount of
substance behind it.

We verified the identity with the pipeline's own substance code, starting
from the report at the median of $\susdens$ (60{,}951 lemmatised tokens,
3{,}984 vocabulary hits on 214 distinct entries, $\susdens = 6.5364$), and
re-vectorising all 343 documents in every run
(Table~\ref{tab:measurement-demos}). \emph{Amplification}: we synthesised a
document from the report's observed counts, writing out each vocabulary entry
as many times as it occurs, separated by a filler token that belongs to no
entry and padded with the same filler, and multiplied the written-out block by
$k \in \{1, 2, 5, 20\}$. The count vector is exactly $k$ times the $k=1$
vector, every $df_j$ is unchanged, and $\suslag$ does not move: its largest
absolute deviation across the four runs is $3.5 \times 10^{-18}$. The
synthetic document registers 4{,}131 hits rather than 3{,}984 because entries
are counted independently (\S\ref{sec:index-notation}). \emph{Dilution}: we
appended 152{,}378 filler tokens, cycled from the report's 50 most frequent
tokens outside the vocabulary, to the real report. Density falls 3.50-fold;
$\suslag$ is bit-for-bit unchanged.

If magnitude is discarded, what remains is dispersion across terms. For a
non-negative unit vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^V$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unit-sum-bounds}
1 \;\leq\; \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{V} u_j \;\leq\; \sqrt{V},
\end{equation}
with the lower bound attained when all mass sits on one term and the upper
bound when it is spread evenly over all $V$; mass spread evenly over $m$ terms
gives $\sqrt{m}$. Aggregating an L2-normalised vector therefore rewards
spreading mentions over many terms, not mentioning them more. On our corpus
the unit sums $V \cdot \suslag_d$ run from $1.93$ to $8.62$ (mean $5.73$) and
correlate $0.9575$ with $\sqrt{\BREADTH_d}$. $\suslag$ itself correlates
$0.9546$ with $\BREADTH$ (Spearman $0.951$) and $0.4052$ with $\susdens$
(Spearman $0.4524$); Figure~\ref{fig:robustness-specs} plots both
relationships. Under L2 vector normalisation the substance component is, to a
correlation of $0.95$, a measure of topical breadth.

The idf weighting is a smaller matter. In a corpus of ESG disclosure the terms
present everywhere are the ones the genre is about, so idf moves weight away
from them: the 49 entries with $\mathrm{idf} < 1.2$ (document frequency above
280) hold 69.26\,\% of the 1{,}047{,}951 ESG occurrences, while the 207
entries with $\mathrm{idf} > 2$ hold 6.70\,\%. One mention of \emph{living
income} (two occurrences in the corpus, $\mathrm{idf} = 5.7420$) weighs as much
as $5.545$ mentions of \emph{board of directors}, the most frequent entry
(59{,}912 occurrences, $\mathrm{idf} = 1.0355$). Because the promoted entries
carry so little mass, the effect on the score is small: holding the length
denominator fixed and varying only the weighting, $\susdens$ and $\sustfidf$
correlate $0.9876$ and the resulting scores have Spearman correlation
$0.9926$; varying the vector normalisation instead lowers it to $0.6421$
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness-index}). We keep uniform weights because they need no
document frequencies: a report's density does not change when the corpus
does.

\subsubsection{Convergent validity with quantitative content}
\label{sec:validity}

The argument above is algebraic. An empirical check needs a yardstick built
by different means, and $\QUANT$ (\S\ref{sec:index-components}) is one: a
density of regular-expression matches over the raw text, with no vectoriser,
no lemmatised vocabulary and no document-frequency weighting. A report that
discloses substance should also quantify it, so a substance measure ought to
covary with $\QUANT$ \citep{campbell1959}. Agreement does not establish
validity, but a substance measure that does not covary with independently
counted quantitative content is hard to defend.

The two instruments are not fully independent. Seventeen of the 399
vocabulary entries are also matched by a $\QUANT$ pattern (reporting-framework
acronyms, the \emph{taxonomy} family, \emph{co2}, \emph{co2e}, \emph{ghg} and
four ISO standards); they carry 5.39\,\% of the vocabulary's occurrences, and
strings of theirs make up 27.31\,\% of $\QUANT$'s 186{,}984 hits. Both
instruments also read the same extracted zones, so the test is conditional on
the extraction stage (\S\ref{sec:extraction}). We therefore report the
comparison twice (Table~\ref{tab:validity-disjoint}): on the full bases, and on
disjoint bases, with the 17 entries removed from the vocabulary (399 to 382)
and $\QUANT$ restricted to its two purely numeric families,
\texttt{percentages} and \texttt{large\_numbers}. The restricted $\QUANT$
correlates $0.8697$ with the full one. Because the 343 reports are seven
reports each of 49 firms, every interval resamples firms, not reports.

On the full bases the two length-normalised specifications correlate with

a ratio of $2.11$; Spearman $0.6804$ against $0.4521$), and the firm-bootstrap

both sides the correlations are $0.5904$ for $\susdens$ and $0.1894$ for

\paragraph{Disjoint bases.}
\label{sec:validity-disjoint}
Every correlation falls, by 37--38\,\%, so the full-basis levels are upper
bounds and convergent validity on clean bases is moderate. The ratio does not
fall: $2.16$ for $\susdens$ against $\suslag$, against $2.11$ on the full
bases, so the shared strings did not produce the ordering. What changes is
the width of the intervals. The difference is $0.2142$ with an interval from $-0.143$ to
$0.519$, and one bootstrap sample in six reverses it; under Spearman the gap
is $0.0821$, interval $[-0.117,\ 0.272]$. The uncertainty sits on the
$\suslag$ side. Across the 49 firm means $\susdens$ correlates $0.5265$ with
the restricted $\QUANT$ and $\suslag$ $0.3400$; within firms, over time,
$0.2071$ against $-0.0716$. On clean bases the direction is the same in every
cut, but a firm-clustered interval does not exclude equality: convergent
validity corroborates the choice of a length-normalised specification without
carrying it on its own. The case against $\suslag$ rests first on
\eqref{eq:l2-invariance} and its demonstrations, which use no yardstick.

\paragraph{Density rather than length-normalised TF-IDF.}
\label{sec:validity-adopted}
Of the two length-normalised specifications, $\sustfidf$ correlates slightly
more strongly with $\QUANT$, by $0.0087$ on the full bases. The firm bootstrap does not distinguish that
gap from zero (interval $[-0.018,\ 0.044]$; 2{,}748 of 9{,}999 samples reverse
it), and on disjoint bases it shrinks to $0.0014$. The criterion separates the
length-normalised pair from $\suslag$, not the two members of the pair. We
adopt $\susdens$ on a structural ground: it estimates nothing on the sample, so
a report's substance score does not depend on which other reports are in the
corpus. A further variant, sublinear term frequency divided by length, fails
the test outright ($r = 0.0500$ with $\QUANT$), because once each term's
contribution is damped towards presence the length denominator dominates the
ratio.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$z$}{z}-scores rather than min--max, and no threshold}
\label{sec:artefact}

\paragraph{What the antecedent computed.}
\label{sec:artefact-which}
The printed formula of \citet[p.~4]{lagasio2024} is a difference of
$z$-scores, but its published numbers are min--max. Its Table~2 reports both
components on $[0,1]$ and a mean index of $-0.2179$, which is exactly the
difference of the two component means, $0.3727 - 0.5906$
\citep[p.~6]{lagasio2024}; a difference of $z$-scores would have mean zero by
construction. The article's stated reason for min--max is to ``address
potential biases due to variations in document length and linguistic style''
(p.~4). A min--max rescaling is monotone within each component, so any
dependence on length present before it is present, in the same rank order,
after it.

\paragraph{What the choice changes.} $\Z(\cdot)$ and the min--max map
$M(\cdot)$ are both increasing affine maps, so within a component they
preserve ranks. Once two components are differenced they are no longer
equivalent, because they equalise the components' spreads differently. On our
corpus, with the adopted substance component, the difference is small in
ranks: the $z$-score and min--max versions of $\ESGSI$ have Spearman
correlation $0.9971$ ($0.9957$ with $\sustfidf$, $0.9888$ with $\suslag$). The
difference is in location. Under $\Z$ the mean of the score is zero by
construction. Under $M$ it is $\overline{M(\SEN)} - \overline{M(\SUS)}$, which
is set by where each component's mass sits inside its own observed range. With
$\susdens$ the two rescaled means are almost equal ($0.3645$ and $0.3610$) and
the score has mean $+0.0035$. With $\suslag$ the rescaled substance mean rises
to $0.5678$ and the score's mean shifts to $-0.2034$, although nothing about
the reports has changed. The antecedent's published mean, $-0.2179$, has the
same structure: a substance distribution sitting high in its range. The
resemblance of the two figures is consistent with that mechanism; on a
different corpus with a different vocabulary it is not evidence of what the
antecedent ran.

\paragraph{Consequences for our specification.} We standardise by $z$-scores
because they fix the location of every component at the corpus mean and
express each in units of its own standard deviation, so that the sign and size
of a score describe a report's position relative to the corpus and nothing
else. Under min--max the location of zero would depend on the extremes of each
component's distribution, which a single outlying report can move. For the
same reason we apply no threshold. Zero is the corpus mean of the combination,
a property of the rescaling and of the corpus; it is not a calibrated boundary
between kinds of disclosure, and there is no external benchmark against which
a boundary could be calibrated (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). The antecedent's
threshold at zero inherits its location from the rescaling, and we neither use
it nor replace it with another.
